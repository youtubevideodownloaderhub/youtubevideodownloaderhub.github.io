Is Downloading YouTube Videos Legal? The Rules, Simply Explained

A plain-English guide to the copyright and terms-of-service rules around saving YouTube videos.

The legality of downloading YouTube videos is one of the most searched questions about the platform, and the answer is less simple than a yes or no. It depends on who owns the video, what licence it carries, how you intend to use the download, and where you live. This guide gives a plain-English overview of the three layers that matter: copyright law, YouTube's terms of service, and the practical reality of enforcement. It is general information, not legal advice, and the rules differ by country.

Copyright: who owns the video

Every video on YouTube is owned by someone, usually the uploader or a studio, label, or publisher they work with. Copyright gives the owner the exclusive right to copy and distribute the work, so downloading a copy without permission is, in strict legal terms, making an unauthorised copy. Exceptions exist in some countries for private copying, research, or criticism, but those exceptions are narrow and vary widely. The United States has fair use, the United Kingdom has fair dealing, and the European Union has its own list of exceptions, and none of them gives a general right to download anything you like.

YouTube's terms of service

Separate from copyright law, YouTube's terms say you may not download content except where YouTube provides a download button or otherwise permits it. That is why YouTube Premium's offline feature is the only sanctioned way to save videos for offline viewing. Breaking the terms is not automatically a crime, but it can lead to account restrictions, and it matters if you ever need YouTube's help with your account. Creators who download their own videos through YouTube Studio are fully within the terms.

When downloading is clearly fine

Downloading your own uploads is always fine. Videos released under a Creative Commons licence are fine to download and reuse within the licence terms, which usually means giving credit. Public-domain material, such as very old films or government-produced videos, is fine too. Educational use can be lawful in some places, but only within the limits of the local exception, and a teacher who wants to show a clip in class is usually better served by streaming it or using YouTube's own tools.

The practical risks

Enforcement against individual viewers is rare, which is why many people assume downloading is harmless, but the risk is not zero and it is not the only risk. Downloader sites are a common source of malware, and re-uploading someone else's video can lead to a copyright strike, a channel termination, or a takedown claim. The safest approach is to use the official offline feature, to download only content you own or that is openly licensed, and to link to videos rather than copying them whenever you share something with others.

What to do if you get a copyright strike

A copyright strike means a rights holder asked YouTube to remove your video, and it is a serious matter for a channel: three strikes lead to termination. If you receive one, read the notice carefully to see which video and which part of it was claimed. If you believe the claim is wrong, for example because the video is your own work or you have a licence, you can submit a counter-notification through YouTube Studio, but be honest, because a false counter-notification has legal consequences. If the claim is correct, delete the video, learn from the mistake, and do not re-upload it. YouTube also offers Copyright School, a short course that removes a strike after completion in some cases. The best protection is prevention: use only content you own or have licensed, keep your licences and permissions in a folder, and never assume that crediting the owner is a substitute for permission.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I download my own YouTube videos?

Yes. Content you created and uploaded is yours to download, and YouTube Studio lets you do exactly that.

What about Creative Commons videos?

Videos marked Creative Commons can be reused under the licence terms, which usually require attribution. Check the exact licence first.

Can I get in trouble for downloading?

Downloading copyrighted material without permission can be infringement, and YouTube can restrict accounts that break its terms. The risk is real even if enforcement is uneven.
